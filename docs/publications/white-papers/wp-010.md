<div class="publication-core-thesis">
  <div class="publication-core-thesis-label">Core Thesis</div>
  <p>Digital colleagues are not people. They are engineered participants in work: non-human identities with defined roles, bounded authority, supplied context, measurable obligations, explicit escalation paths, and accountable human ownership. Trust depends less on how human an agent appears than on how deliberately the work, interfaces, controls, evidence, and oversight are designed.</p>
</div>

## Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence is moving beyond individual productivity assistance and into active organizational work. Coding and enterprise agents can accept assignments, inspect systems, use tools, coordinate steps, maintain state, produce artifacts, and return results for review. This creates a new operating model in which people increasingly define intent, supply context, set boundaries, review evidence, and remain accountable while agents perform bounded portions of the work.

WP-010 argues that the language of “digital colleagues” is useful only when it is engineered precisely. An agent is not a person, employee, professional, or moral actor. It is a non-human system occupying a defined role in a workflow under authority delegated by people and institutions. The paper therefore treats human-agent collaboration as a systems-engineering problem involving work architecture, role design, decision rights, delegation contracts, shared context, identity and access, review, escalation, evaluation, memory, observability, and organizational learning.

A central design rule is to begin with the work rather than the agent platform. Teams should determine which outcomes require human purpose, judgment, legitimacy, and ownership of consequences; which tasks can be delegated or automated; what authority is permitted; what evidence must be produced; when approval is required; and how authority can be revoked. Digital colleagues should have traceable non-human identities, scoped credentials, explicit handoff protocols, meaningful human oversight, and technically enforced limits.

The paper also examines how digital colleagues change software engineering, organizational design, workforce development, and management. It introduces failure modes such as anthropomorphic overtrust, unbounded delegation, accountability laundering, shadow agent workforces, rubber-stamp oversight, self-validation, and displaced learning. It concludes with a human-agent operating model and a five-level maturity model in which authority expands or contracts through evidence rather than assumption.

The ETIS position is that digital colleagues should be engineered as bounded participants in trustworthy systems. Their work should enter the same repository-centered, evidence-centered, governed review environment as human work. Agents may increase capacity and speed, but humans and organizations remain responsible for purpose, supervision, consequential decisions, operational outcomes, and stewardship.

## Why Read This Paper?

WP-010 provides a practical engineering model for designing human-agent teams without confusing machine capability with human accountability. It is especially useful for organizations moving from AI assistance toward agentic execution, multi-agent workflows, or persistent non-human participants in engineering and enterprise work.

After reading it, you should be able to:

- define a digital colleague as an engineered role rather than a simulated person;
- redesign work so that human judgment and machine capability reinforce each other;
- specify decision rights, approval gates, evidence obligations, escalation triggers, and revocation through a delegation contract;
- design shared context and reliable handoffs for human-agent and multi-agent work;
- establish meaningful human oversight that can understand, challenge, and intervene;
- govern non-human identities, credentials, authorization, lifecycle, and separation of duties;
- evaluate digital-colleague performance at the level of outcomes, quality, governance, reliability, efficiency, and collaboration;
- distinguish governed organizational learning from uncontrolled agent memory;
- recognize common forms of “colleague theater” and accountability failure;
- design a scalable human-agent operating model and assess organizational maturity.

## What the Paper Examines

1. The shift from AI tool use to digital colleagues as active participants in organizational work.
2. What a digital colleague is—and is not—including the limits of anthropomorphic framing.
3. Work architecture and the deliberate division of labor between humans and agents.
4. Roles, decision rights, delegation contracts, approval gates, evidence, escalation, and revocation.
5. Shared context, handoffs, interaction protocols, and multi-agent coordination.
6. Meaningful human oversight, review, intervention, and accountability.
7. Non-human identity, least privilege, credential lifecycle, separation of duties, containment, and inventory.
8. Outcome-based evaluation, memory, feedback, organizational learning, and workforce implications.
9. Digital colleagues in software engineering and the failure modes of “colleague theater.”
10. A human-agent team operating model and five-level digital-colleague maturity model.
